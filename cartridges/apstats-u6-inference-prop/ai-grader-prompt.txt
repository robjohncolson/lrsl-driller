6.1 The Logic of Significance Testing:
- When we observe a sample result, there are always TWO possible explanations:
  (1) The result happened by CHANCE alone (random variation)
  (2) There is a REAL effect or difference (the claim is true)
- A simulation or randomization distribution shows what results are expected by chance alone
- If the observed result is UNLIKELY to happen by chance (small probability, typically < 5%), we have CONVINCING EVIDENCE for the claim
- If the observed result COULD plausibly happen by chance (probability not small), we do NOT have convincing evidence
- The probability from the simulation tells us how often results as extreme as (or more extreme than) the observed result occur by chance alone
- "Convincing evidence" does NOT mean "proof" — it means the chance explanation is unlikely
- Common reasoning errors:
  - Confusing a small probability with a large one (e.g., claiming evidence IS convincing when 40% of simulations produced the result — that's NOT convincing)
  - Failing to reference the actual probability from the simulation
  - Not connecting the probability to whether the result is likely or unlikely by chance
  - Saying "the data proves..." instead of "the data provides evidence..."

6.1 Convincing Evidence Explanations (convincingExplain):
- A complete explanation MUST include:
  (1) Reference to the simulation probability (e.g., "only 2 out of 200 simulations" or "1% of simulations")
  (2) A statement about whether this probability makes the result likely or unlikely to occur by chance alone
  (3) A logical conclusion connecting to the claim (e.g., "so we have convincing evidence that..." or "so we do not have convincing evidence that...")
- E: All three elements present — references probability, connects to chance, logical conclusion
- P: Mentions probability but weak reasoning, OR good reasoning but wrong/missing probability reference, OR correct conclusion but incomplete justification
- I: No reference to simulation probability, OR claims evidence is convincing when probability is HIGH (or vice versa), OR fundamentally flawed logic
- Example of E response: "Because only 3 out of 200 simulations (1.5%) produced a sample proportion as extreme as 0.72, it is unlikely that the observed result happened by chance alone. This provides convincing evidence that the true proportion is greater than 0.60."
- Example of P response: "The probability is small so the evidence is convincing." (missing specific probability value and context)
- Example of I response: "Since 150 out of 200 simulations were as extreme, this is convincing evidence." (high probability = NOT convincing, logic is backwards)

6.2 One-Sample z-Interval for a Population Proportion:
- Purpose: Estimate the population proportion p using a confidence interval based on the sample proportion p-hat
- Formula: p-hat +/- z* x sqrt(p-hat(1-p-hat)/n)
- Components: point estimate (p-hat), critical value (z*), standard error SE(p-hat) = sqrt(p-hat(1-p-hat)/n), margin of error (z* x SE)
- Common z* values: 90% -> 1.645, 95% -> 1.960, 99% -> 2.576
- The confidence LEVEL determines z*; higher confidence = wider interval

6.2 Conditions for Inference (conditionsExplain):
- Three conditions must ALL be verified:
  (1) RANDOM: The data come from a random sample or randomized experiment
  (2) 10% CONDITION: The sample size n is less than or equal to 10% of the population N (n <= 0.10N) — ensures approximate independence
  (3) LARGE COUNTS: np-hat >= 10 AND n(1-p-hat) >= 10 — ensures the sampling distribution is approximately normal
- A complete explanation MUST:
  - Address ALL THREE conditions (not just one or two)
  - Show numerical verification where applicable (plug in actual numbers for 10% and Large Counts)
  - State a correct conclusion for each condition (met or not met)
- E: Addresses all three conditions with specific numbers, correct conclusion about each, correct overall conclusion
- P: Addresses some conditions correctly but misses one, OR correct conclusions but missing numerical verification, OR addresses all three but one conclusion is wrong
- I: Misidentifies which conditions to check, wrong conclusion on two or more, OR only addresses one condition
- Example of E response: "Random: The problem states this is a random sample of 150 residents, so the random condition is met. 10%: 150 is less than 10% of all residents in the city (150 < 0.10 x 50,000 = 5,000), so the 10% condition is met. Large Counts: np-hat = 150(0.62) = 93 >= 10 and n(1-p-hat) = 150(0.38) = 57 >= 10, so the Large Counts condition is met. All conditions are satisfied."
- Example of P response: "It's a random sample, and np-hat and n(1-p-hat) are both at least 10, so conditions are met." (missing 10% condition, no numbers shown)
- Example of I response: "The sample size is large enough because n > 30." (wrong condition entirely — n > 30 is for means/CLT, not for proportion inference)
- Common student errors:
  - Confusing the Large Counts condition (np-hat >= 10 and n(1-p-hat) >= 10) with the CLT condition (n >= 30) — these are DIFFERENT
  - Using the population proportion p in Large Counts instead of the sample proportion p-hat (for confidence intervals, we use p-hat because p is unknown)
  - Forgetting to check the 10% condition entirely
  - Stating conditions are met without showing the numerical check
  - Checking only one of the two Large Counts inequalities

6.2 Standard Error:
- SE(p-hat) = sqrt(p-hat(1-p-hat)/n)
- Uses p-hat (sample proportion), NOT p (population proportion), because p is unknown when constructing a CI
- Common errors: forgetting the square root, dividing by n outside the radical, using p instead of p-hat

6.2 Margin of Error:
- ME = z* x SE(p-hat) = z* x sqrt(p-hat(1-p-hat)/n)
- Larger z* (higher confidence) -> larger ME -> wider interval
- Larger n -> smaller SE -> smaller ME -> narrower interval

6.2 Minimum Sample Size:
- Solve n >= p-hat(1-p-hat) x (z*/ME)^2
- If no prior estimate of p is available, use p-hat = 0.5 (maximizes the product p-hat(1-p-hat), gives the most conservative/largest sample size)
- ALWAYS round UP to the next whole number (you can't survey a fraction of a person)

6.2 Confidence Interval Interpretation (capstoneExplain):
- A correct interpretation MUST include ALL THREE elements:
  (1) The CONFIDENCE LEVEL (e.g., "We are 95% confident...")
  (2) The INTERVAL with endpoints or reference to "from ___ to ___"
  (3) The POPULATION PARAMETER IN CONTEXT (e.g., "the true proportion of adults in this city who support the policy")
- Template: "We are __% confident that the interval from ___ to ___ captures the true proportion of [population description] who [context]."
- E: All three elements present with correct context and parameter description
- P: Missing one element (e.g., no confidence level, or generic "true proportion" without context), OR all elements present but imprecise language
- I: Major misinterpretation — any of the following fatal errors:
  - "There is a 95% PROBABILITY that the true proportion is in this interval" (WRONG — the interval either captures p or it doesn't; probability applies to the METHOD, not a single interval)
  - "95% of all samples will fall in this interval" (WRONG — confuses samples with the parameter)
  - "We accept that p = ..." (WRONG — CI gives a range, not a point value)
  - "This proves that the true proportion is..." (WRONG — statistics provides evidence, not proof)
  - Interpreting the interval as being about individual observations rather than the population parameter
- The word "CONFIDENT" is critical — it refers to the long-run success rate of the method. If we constructed many 95% CIs from repeated samples, about 95% of them would capture the true parameter.
- Common student errors:
  - Using "probability" instead of "confident" — this is the single most common and most serious error
  - Describing the sample statistic instead of the population parameter
  - Omitting context (just saying "the true proportion" without specifying of WHAT and for WHOM)
  - Saying "between" instead of "from ___ to ___" is acceptable, but the endpoints must be referenced
  - Saying the parameter "is" in the interval with certainty, rather than that the interval "captures" or "contains" it

6.4 Setting Up a Test for a Population Proportion:

6.4 Null Hypothesis (nullHypothesis, cap64Null):
- H0 ALWAYS contains "=" and uses the population parameter p (NOT p-hat)
- H0: p = p0, where p0 is the claimed/null value
- E: Correctly states H0: p = p0 with the right p0 value
- P: Right structure but wrong p0 value or minor notation error
- I: Uses p-hat, uses inequality, or fundamentally wrong

6.4 Alternative Hypothesis (altHypothesis, cap64Alt):
- Ha uses a strict inequality: < , > , or ≠ (never =)
- Direction determined by the RESEARCH QUESTION, not the data:
  - "greater than" / "more" / "higher" / "increased" → Ha: p > p0
  - "less than" / "fewer" / "lower" / "decreased" → Ha: p < p0
  - "different from" / "differs" / "changed" → Ha: p ≠ p0
- E: Correct direction and p0 value, uses p (not p-hat)
- P: Correct p0 but wrong direction, or minor notation issues
- I: Uses p-hat, uses equality, or fundamentally wrong

6.4 Parameter Definition (paramDef, cap64ParamDef):
- Must define p using POPULATION language: "the proportion of ALL [population] who WOULD [action]"
- Key words: "all", "every", "would" indicate population (good)
- Bad sample language: "surveyed", "said", "in the sample" (these are about the sample, not population)
- E: Includes "proportion" + population language ("all", "would") + context
- P: Mentions proportion and context but uses sample language
- I: Missing proportion, missing context, or completely wrong

6.4 Identify Test Procedure (testAnswer, cap64Procedure):
- One sample + categorical data + testing a claim → One-sample z-test for a population proportion
- Common errors: choosing z-INTERVAL (that's for estimating, not testing), t-test (that's for means), two-sample (only one group here)
- E: Selects "One-sample z-test for a population proportion"
- I: Any other choice

6.4 Test Conditions (testConditionsExplain, cap64ConditionsWork):
- Three conditions: Random, 10%, Large Counts
- CRITICAL DISTINCTION: Test conditions use p0 (null value), NOT p-hat!
  - CI conditions: np-hat ≥ 10 and n(1-p-hat) ≥ 10
  - TEST conditions: np0 ≥ 10 and n(1-p0) ≥ 10
  - Why? When testing, we ASSUME H0 is true, so we use p0
- E: Checks all 3 conditions with calculations using p0 (not p-hat)
- P: Checks some conditions, or checks all but uses p-hat instead of p0
- I: Missing most conditions or completely wrong approach

6.4 Hypothesis Error Detection (errorAnswer):
- Common student errors to identify:
  1. Using p-hat instead of p in hypotheses
  2. Putting inequality in H0 (H0 must use =)
  3. Using equality in Ha (Ha must use <, >, or ≠)
  4. Wrong direction for Ha (doesn't match research question)
  5. Defining parameter with sample language instead of population language
  6. Using wrong p0 value

6.5 Calculating the Test Statistic (zStatAnswer, cap65ZStat):
- Formula: z = (p-hat - p0) / sqrt(p0(1-p0)/n)
- CRITICAL: The denominator uses p0 (null value), NOT p-hat!
- Why? We assume H0 is true when calculating the test statistic
- The z-score tells us how many standard deviations p-hat is from p0
- E: Correct z-value (within rounding tolerance of 0.02)
- P: Close but arithmetic error (within 0.10)
- I: Wrong formula, used p-hat in denominator, or major calculation error
- Common errors:
  - Using p-hat instead of p0 in the standard deviation (denominator)
  - Forgetting to subtract p0 in the numerator (dividing p-hat by SD instead of (p-hat - p0) by SD)
  - Forgetting to take the square root in the denominator

6.5 Calculating the p-Value (pvalueAnswer, cap65PValue):
- The p-value depends on the direction of Ha:
  - Ha: p > p0 → P(Z >= z), area in the RIGHT tail (one-sided)
  - Ha: p < p0 → P(Z <= z), area in the LEFT tail (one-sided)
  - Ha: p ≠ p0 → 2 × P(Z >= |z|), area in BOTH tails (two-sided)
- A p-value must be between 0 and 1
- E: Correct p-value (within tolerance of 0.002)
- P: Used one tail instead of two (or vice versa), close but wrong calculation
- I: Wrong tail entirely, p-value > 1, or major error
- Common errors:
  - For two-sided tests: finding only one tail area and forgetting to double
  - For one-sided tests: doubling the area when they shouldn't
  - Using the wrong tail direction

6.5 Interpreting a p-Value (pvalueInterpretation, cap65Interpret):
- A correct interpretation MUST include ALL of these elements:
  (1) ASSUME H0 IS TRUE: "Assuming that [null hypothesis in context]..."
  (2) THE PROBABILITY: "...there is a [p-value] probability..."
  (3) SAMPLE RESULT OR MORE EXTREME: "...of getting a sample proportion of [p-hat] or [greater/less/more extreme]..."
  (4) BY CHANCE ALONE: "...by chance alone..."
  (5) CONTEXT: "...in a random sample of [n] [units] from [population]."
- For two-sided tests, say "as extreme as or more extreme in either direction"
- For one-sided tests, say "or greater" (for >) or "or less" (for <)
- FATAL ERROR: "The p-value is the probability that H0 is true" → WRONG
  - The p-value is calculated ASSUMING H0 is true; it is NOT the probability of H0
- Template (one-sided, >): "Assuming that [p0]% of [population] [action], there is a [p-value] probability of getting a sample proportion of [p-hat] or greater by chance alone in a random sample of [n]."
- Template (two-sided): "Assuming that [p0]% of [population] [action], there is a [p-value] probability of getting a sample proportion as extreme as or more extreme than [p-hat] in either direction by chance alone in a random sample of [n]."
- E: All five elements present, correct context, no misconceptions
- P: Missing one or two elements, or imprecise language (e.g., missing "or more extreme")
- I: States p-value is probability H0 is true, missing most elements, or fundamentally wrong

6.5 Test Direction (directionAnswer):
- Ha: p > p0 → RIGHT tail (one-sided, upper)
- Ha: p < p0 → LEFT tail (one-sided, lower)
- Ha: p ≠ p0 → BOTH tails (two-sided), p-value = 2 × one-tail area
- NEVER find area "between" -z and z for a p-value
- E: Correct tail direction matching Ha
- I: Wrong tail or wrong number of tails

6.3 Justifying a Claim (claimAnswer, claimExplain, cap63Justify, cap63JustifyExplain):
- Rule: If ALL values in the CI are consistent with the claim → convincing evidence
- Rule: If ONE OR MORE values are inconsistent → NOT convincing evidence
- Example: CI = (0.52, 0.68), claim: "majority support" (>0.5). ALL values > 0.5 → convincing evidence
- Example: CI = (0.47, 0.56), claim: "majority support" (>0.5). 0.47 < 0.5 → NOT convincing evidence
- E: Correctly references "all values" or "some values" in the interval, connects to claim, gives conclusion
- P: Correct conclusion but weak reasoning, or good reasoning but wrong conclusion
- I: Backwards logic, no reference to interval values, or treats p-hat alone as sufficient

6.3 Confidence Level Interpretation (confLevelAnswer):
- CORRECT: "In repeated random sampling with the same sample size, approximately C% of C% confidence intervals will capture the population proportion."
- WRONG: "There is a C% probability this interval contains the true proportion" (applies to method, not single interval)
- WRONG: "C% of the population data falls within this interval"
- WRONG: "The sample proportion is within C% of the true proportion"
- Key distinction: Once calculated, a specific interval either captures p (probability = 1) or doesn't (probability = 0)

6.3 Factors Affecting Margin of Error (factorAnswer):
- ME = z* × √(p̂(1−p̂)/n)
- Increasing sample size n → DECREASES ME (n is in denominator under square root)
- Increasing confidence level → INCREASES z* → INCREASES ME
- Width of CI = 2 × ME
- To halve ME: must QUADRUPLE n (because ME is proportional to 1/√n)
- Width proportional to 1/√n

6.6 Concluding a Test for a Population Proportion:
- The significance level (alpha) is a predetermined boundary for judging whether a p-value is "small"
- Common significance levels: alpha = 0.05, alpha = 0.01, alpha = 0.10
- If p-value <= alpha: REJECT H0 — there IS convincing statistical evidence for Ha
- If p-value > alpha: FAIL TO REJECT H0 — there is NOT convincing statistical evidence for Ha
- Conclusions must explicitly compare p-value to alpha (e.g., "Because the p-value of 0.03 < alpha = 0.05")
- Conclusions must be about the ALTERNATIVE hypothesis, stated in context
- If no alpha is given in the problem, use alpha = 0.05 (most common default)

6.6 Common Conclusion Errors to Detect:
- FATAL: "Accept the null hypothesis" — NEVER accept H0; we "fail to reject" H0
- FATAL: "We have proven that Ha is true" — statistics never proves; it provides evidence
- FATAL: "Accept the alternative hypothesis" — we say "convincing evidence for Ha," not "accept Ha"
- SERIOUS: Failing to explicitly compare p-value to alpha (must say "because p-value ___ is [less/greater] than alpha = ___")
- SERIOUS: Concluding about H0 instead of Ha (e.g., "the proportion IS 0.5" instead of "there is not convincing evidence that the proportion differs from 0.5")
- SERIOUS: Stating result as fact when we should say "convincing evidence" (e.g., "the rate is less than 10%" vs "there is convincing evidence that the rate is less than 10%")
- MODERATE: Missing context in the conclusion (must state Ha in terms of the specific scenario)
- MODERATE: Contradicting the decision (rejecting H0 but saying "no evidence," or failing to reject but saying "convincing evidence")
- MODERATE: Stating the conclusion about the wrong direction (e.g., Ha is two-sided but conclusion states one-sided result)

6.6 Writing a Complete Conclusion (conclusionText, fullTestConclusion, cap66Conclusion):
- A complete conclusion MUST include FOUR elements:
  (1) EXPLICIT COMPARISON: "Because the p-value of [value] is [less than / greater than] alpha = [value]"
  (2) DECISION: "we [reject / fail to reject] H0"
  (3) EVIDENCE STATEMENT: "There [is / is not] convincing statistical evidence that..."
  (4) CONTEXT: State Ha in terms of the real-world scenario
- Template (reject): "Because the p-value of ___ is less than alpha = ___, we reject H0. There is convincing statistical evidence that [Ha in context]."
- Template (fail to reject): "Because the p-value of ___ is greater than alpha = ___, we fail to reject H0. There is not convincing statistical evidence that [Ha in context]."
- E: All four elements present, correct decision, no misconceptions
- P: Missing one element (usually the explicit comparison), OR correct elements but imprecise language, OR "accept Ha" instead of "convincing evidence"
- I: Wrong decision, "accept H0," "proven," contradicts decision, or missing most elements
- Example of E response (reject): "Because the p-value of 0.024 is less than alpha = 0.10, we reject H0. There is convincing statistical evidence that the proportion of all adults in this town who would say football is their favorite sport differs from 0.40."
- Example of E response (fail to reject): "Because the p-value of 0.1357 is greater than alpha = 0.05, we fail to reject H0. There is not convincing statistical evidence that more than half of all students at this school would choose the green cup."
- Example of P response: "We reject H0. There is convincing evidence the proportion differs from 0.40." (missing explicit comparison)
- Example of I response: "Because the p-value is small, we accept the null hypothesis." (wrong decision verb + missing comparison)
